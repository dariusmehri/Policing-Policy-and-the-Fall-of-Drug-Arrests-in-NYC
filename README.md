# Policing Policy and the Fall of Drug Arrests in NYC

Drug arrests in New York City have declined sharply over the past two decades, driven largely by policy and enforcement changes rather than a clear reduction in drug activity. A key turning point was the scaling back of Stop-and-Frisk following the court ruling in Floyd v. City of New York, which led to a dramatic drop in police stops and the low-level drug arrests that often resulted from them (New York Civil Liberties Union).

At the same time, marijuana enforcement collapsed due to policy changes culminating in legalization under the Marijuana Regulation and Taxation Act. Because marijuana possession had previously accounted for a large share of arrests, this shift alone eliminated tens of thousands of cases annually (New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services).

Policing strategy also evolved, moving away from “broken windows” enforcement toward a greater focus on serious and violent crime. This change reduced the emphasis on low-level offenses, including minor drug possession, and contributed to the overall decline in arrests (Brennan Center for Justice; Vera Institute of Justice).

Finally, broader criminal justice reforms—such as bail reform and shifts in prosecutorial practices that deprioritized minor offenses—further reduced both arrests and case processing for low-level drug crimes (New York State Legislature; Data Collaborative for Justice at John Jay College).
